Observed-data heat fits with numerical cross-checks

A completed linear solve does not establish that a graph smoother is accurate. The default numerical.policy = "conservative" therefore retains the spectral, heat-time and missing-block condition checks. An opt-in policy, "verified.fallback", offers a bounded alternative when the latter two checks refuse a calculation. It checks every requested positive time and observed response mask, even when the conservative checks would pass. It reports which calculations needed the fallback. It does not weaken the initial requirement that the graph’s positive eigenvalues be numerically distinguishable from its known nullspace.

The fallback currently supports a complete spectrum, the unnormalized Laplacian, the heat filter, and a fixed tuning grid. It uses two calculations in double precision. Their agreement is evidence for the tested calculation, not an interval certificate or a guarantee for arbitrary graphs. It costs an additional dense eigendecomposition and additional matrix solves. Partial spectra, normalized Laplacians, other filters and automatic grid expansion are outside this implementation’s scope.

The fitted model

Let \(L\) be the unnormalized graph Laplacian and \(H_t=\exp(-tL)\) its heat operator. For fully observed responses the fitted vector is \(H_ty\). For observed vertices \(O\), unobserved vertices \(U\), and \(q=|O|\), the observed-data extension minimizes

\[\|y_O-f_O\|^2+f^T\{\exp(tL)-I\}f,\qquad t>0.\]

Each connected component must have an observed response. A completely unobserved component is unidentified and causes an error. With only one observation in each component the extension is componentwise constant and GCV is undefined; the path is available but cannot tune a time.

Directly forming \(\exp(tL)\) can overflow. Instead, put \(A=(I-H_t)/s\), where \(s=\max_i(1-\exp(-t\lambda_i))\) and \(\lambda_i\) are the Laplacian eigenvalues. The implementation computes the small complements with -expm1(-t * lambda). Missing-block elimination gives

\[X=A_{UU}^{-1}A_{UO},\qquad K=A_{OO}-A_{OU}X,\] \[\widehat f_U=-Xy_O,\qquad \widehat f_O=(I-sK)y_O.\]

The implementation evaluates this Schur complement through a residual factor. Write \(A=F^TF\), with \(F=\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{(1-\exp(-t\lambda_i))/s})V^T\) for the checked eigenvector matrix \(V\). Then

\[R=F_O-F_UX,\qquad K=R^TR.\]

This is the same matrix in exact arithmetic. It avoids subtracting two large, nearly equal matrices when the observed vertices are separated by a weak edge. That subtraction can corrupt a small residual trace and hence GCV even when predictions and the missing-block solve appear accurate. The factor calculation adds no ridge or denominator adjustment. The independent bordered reference and all acceptance tolerances below remain unchanged.

Scaling cancels from GCV, giving a stable expression even when \(t\) is very small:

\[\operatorname{df}=q-s\operatorname{tr}(K),\qquad \operatorname{GCV}=q\frac{\|Ky_O\|^2}{\operatorname{tr}(K)^2}.\]

The sample size here is the number of observed responses, not the number of vertices. The identity endpoint \(t=0\) is returned separately with undefined GCV and unknown values still missing. There is no denominator floor, ridge, edge removal or removal of a failed positive grid point.

The alternative calculation and acceptance checks

A separate base-R LAPACK eigendecomposition starts from the actual Laplacian. Both eigensystems undergo the existing nullspace, residual and orthogonality checks. The alternative route constructs \(P=A^+\), the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of its own normalized heat complement. With \(Q\) the component constant orthonormal basis, it solves

\[\begin{pmatrix}P_{OO}&Q_O\\Q_O^T&0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}K_{\mathrm{ref}}\\C\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}I_q\\0\end{pmatrix}.\]

It predicts unobserved responses with \((P_{UO}K_{\mathrm{ref}}+Q_U C)y_O\) and observed responses with \((I-s_{\mathrm{ref}}K_{\mathrm{ref}})y_O\). Thus the two routes eliminate different blocks. When all responses are observed the reference simply applies its own heat operator; neither block solve is necessary.

Acceptance requires all these checks, for every positive time and distinct observed-vertex mask:

Check Maximum accepted value
Candidate missing-block scaled solve residual \(10^{-8}\)
Reference bordered scaled solve residual \(10^{-9}\)
Maximum absolute row sum of prediction-operator difference \(10^{-7}\)
Maximum entry of error preserving the component nullspace, either operator \(10^{-7}\)
Maximum prediction difference for centered, training-RMS-scaled responses \(10^{-7}\)
Absolute effective-degrees-of-freedom difference \(10^{-7}\)
Standardized-response GCV difference \(10^{-10}+10^{-7}|\operatorname{GCV}_{\mathrm{ref}}|\)
Final original-unit GCV difference used for tuning \(10^{-10}+10^{-7}|\operatorname{GCV}^{\mathrm{original}}_{\mathrm{ref}}|\)

For a solve \(BX=C\), its scaled residual is \(\max|BX-C|/\max(1,\max|C|,\|B\|_\infty\max|X|)\). The reference uses the analogous expression with an identity-and-zero right hand side. A nonfinite check, a failed solve, unresolved residual degrees of freedom, or a failed tolerance stops the entire path. All returned positive-time predictions and valid GCV scores must be finite.

The final scores use the same centered calculation as the numerical check. For each response, subtract its observed mean within each connected component, then divide by its training root-mean-square deviation \(\sigma\). Means are calculated relative to an observed anchor in that component, avoiding summing a large additive baseline. Write \(y_O=c_O+\sigma z\), where \(c\) is constant within each component. Since \(Kc_O=0\) and the prediction operator \(T\) preserves those component constants, the package computes

\[\widehat f=c+\sigma Tz,\qquad \operatorname{GCV}_{\mathrm{original}}=\sigma^2\operatorname{GCV}(z).\]

These are algebraic identities, not a change of regression model. The same observed component means and scale are used for the independent calculation. Both the standardized scores and these final original-unit scores must pass their stated agreement tests before being returned or used to select a time. The implementation never recomputes GCV by multiplying \(K\) by the uncentered measurements. gcv.scaled.error records the standardized comparison, while gcv.final.scaled.error records the final-score comparison; each is divided by its allowed tolerance and must not exceed one. A response constant within every component uses scale one and has exactly zero residual score whenever residual degrees of freedom exist. Component means use observed training measurements only. The operator nullspace-preservation check remains mandatory.

numerical$diagnostics retains both conservative diagnostics and agreement checks. response.mask maps response columns to distinct masks; matrix factorizations are shared for responses with the same mask. The declared limits and reference eigensystem diagnostics are also returned. These two computations can share floating-point limitations; no claim of arbitrary forward accuracy follows from their agreement.

Score accuracy and time selection are different decisions

Passing a numerical score check does not imply that a difference between two scores is resolved. For example, two scores \(1-7\times10^{-8}\) and \(1+7\times10^{-8}\) are each within approximately \(10^{-7}\) of the same exact value. Their difference exceeds \(10^{-7}\); it still provides no evidence that one time is better. The verified fallback therefore uses a separate selection rule, without changing any numerical acceptance limit.

At time \(j\), let \(c_j\) and \(r_j\) be the checked candidate and reference scores in original response units, and define the per-score resolution radius \(e(x)=10^{-10}+10^{-7}|x|\). The combined band covers both formulations:

\[\ell_j=\max\{0,\min(c_j-e(c_j),r_j-e(r_j))\},\qquad u_j=\max\{c_j+e(c_j),r_j+e(r_j)\}.\]

These are operational numerical-resolution bands, not statistical confidence intervals or certified enclosures of exact values. Their scale is explicitly tied to the existing pointwise score budgets. When the formulations agree exactly, comparing two times accounts for the sum of their two budgets, not just one budget. Using the envelope of both formulations also prevents a nominal preference in one calculation from concealing disagreement in the other.

Automatic selection among distinct times requires a single time \(j\) satisfying

\[u_j < \min_{k:\,t_k\ne t_j}\ell_k.\]

The separation must be strict: overlapping or touching bands do not justify selection. Duplicate copies of a time are one alternative, with their bands enveloped and all original indices retained. With one prescribed distinct time, application remains available and is labeled single_candidate; there is no claim of an identified optimum.

The returned tuning record provides:

  • unique_grid_minimum only when a time is separated from every alternative. Endpoint flags remain explicit. bracketed means interior to the supplied grid, not proven optimal over continuous time.
  • unresolved_flat_gcv if all bands share a common value, or structural flatness is known as described below. No time is selected.
  • multiple_near_minima if several possible minima remain, even if other times are clearly worse. No time is selected. This is stricter than the earlier behavior that chose the smallest nominal score in this situation.
  • no_valid_candidate when there are no valid positive scores.

near.minimum.indices contains every index whose time has a lower band endpoint no greater than the smallest upper endpoint, except known structurally flat paths, for which every valid time is retained. selection.allowed, structural.flat.reason, resolution (both scores, their radii and combined bands), and separation.margin make the decision inspectable. Raw candidate and reference minimum indices are diagnostics, not selected smoothing times. The reference score matrix is also in numerical$reference.gcv.scores.

Both unresolved statuses stop automatic selection in fit() and automatically tuned refit(). The path and a fixed-time application remain available.

Structurally flat cases do not depend on rounding

If there are \(q\) observations and \(c\) connected components, all observed, the observed residual space has dimension \(q-c\). When \(q-c=1\), its direction is fixed and its residual matrix has the form \(K=\kappa vv^T\) for a fixed unit contrast \(v\). Hence

\[\operatorname{GCV}=q\frac{\|\kappa vv^Ty\|^2}{\kappa^2} =q(v^Ty)^2,\]

which is independent of time. Two observations on a connected graph give the familiar special case \((y_1-y_2)^2\). Such paths are marked structurally flat regardless of their numerical spread and cannot tune a time. Responses constant within each observed component likewise have exactly zero residual score and are structurally flat when GCV is defined. When \(q=c\), residual degrees of freedom vanish and GCV remains undefined instead.

These structural checks accompany, rather than replace, all numerical acceptance checks. An inaccurate or nonfinite path still fails. The absolute part of the score budget has squared-response units; changing units can still change whether a numerical difference is resolved. This is distinct from the additive-shift invariance provided by componentwise centering.
